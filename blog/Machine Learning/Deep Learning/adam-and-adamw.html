Adam and AdamW

Machine Learning

A concise handwritten version of this note is available as a PDF.

If you open the training config of basically any modern LLM -- GPT, Llama, whatever -- the optimizer is AdamW, not Adam. That "W" bugged me for a while. Adam is already one of the reasons deep neural networks can be trained (relatively) easily, so what was broken enough that everyone switched? It turns out Adam has a critical issue when combined with L2 loss, one of the most common regularization methods. The fix is a one-line change, but the reasoning behind it is a nice little story about how two things that look equivalent on paper stop being equivalent the moment you change the optimizer.

(If you want the build-up of how Adam itself works -- momentum, adaptive learning rates and all that -- I cover it in From SGD to Adam. Here I'll take Adam as given.)

Observation 1: L2 loss in SGD is just weight decay

Let's start with plain SGD. Suppose we add an L2 penalty to our loss:

L*(θ) = L(θ) + (λ/2)‖θ‖²

where L(θ) is the original loss and the second term is the L2 regularization. Taking the gradient:

∇L*(θ) = ∇L(θ) + λθ

Now plug this into the SGD update and watch what happens:

θ⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾ ← θ⁽ᵗ⁾ - η∇L*(θ⁽ᵗ⁾)
       = θ⁽ᵗ⁾ - η∇L(θ⁽ᵗ⁾) - ηλθ⁽ᵗ⁾
       = (1 - ηλ)θ⁽ᵗ⁾ - η∇L(θ⁽ᵗ⁾)

Here we are. The L2 penalty has turned into the factor (1 - ηλ) multiplying θ⁽ᵗ⁾ -- every step, the weights get shrunk slightly towards zero before the gradient update is applied. This is exactly weight decay. In SGD, "add L2 loss" and "decay the weights each step" are literally the same thing, which is why people use the two terms interchangeably. That interchangeability is precisely what breaks next.

Observation 2: L2 loss in Adam does NOT give weight decay

Now do the same thing with Adam. We naively feed the regularized gradient ∇L*(θ) into the algorithm:

Adam + L2 loss
1. m⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾ ← β₁m⁽ᵗ⁾ + (1-β₁) ∇L*(θ⁽ᵗ⁾)
2. s⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾ ← β₂s⁽ᵗ⁾ + (1-β₂) ∇L*(θ⁽ᵗ⁾) ⊙ ∇L*(θ⁽ᵗ⁾)
3. m̂⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾ ← m⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾ / (1 - β₁ᵗ)
4. ŝ⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾ ← s⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾ / (1 - β₂ᵗ)
5. θ⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾ ← θ⁽ᵗ⁾ - η m̂⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾ ⊘ (√ŝ⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾ + ε)

Where's the problem? Look at step 2 and step 5 together. The λθ term now lives inside m̂, but m̂ gets divided by √ŝ -- Adam's whole trick of scaling each parameter's update by its gradient magnitude.

Think about what L2 regularization is supposed to do: parameters with large values should feel a large pull back towards zero (their penalty gradient λθ is large). But a large λθ also inflates that parameter's second moment ŝ, and dividing by √ŝ scales the update back down. Adam scales down exactly the gradients that the regularizer made large. The parameters that most deserve to be decayed get their decay largely cancelled out by the adaptive scaling. The regularization effect you thought you were adding mostly evaporates.

This is the subtle part that I think trips people up: nothing errors, nothing diverges, the loss curve looks fine. You just quietly get much weaker regularization than your λ suggests -- and tuning λ and η becomes weirdly entangled.

The fix: AdamW decouples the two

The AdamW solution is almost embarrassingly simple: separate Adam from the L2 loss. Two changes:

  • In steps 1 and 2, use the plain gradient ∇L(θ) instead of ∇L*(θ) -- so the regularization never touches the moment estimates, and the adaptive scaling can't dilute it.
  • In step 5, subtract ηλθ directly -- applying the decay straight to the parameters, outside the adaptive machinery.
AdamW
1. m⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾ ← β₁m⁽ᵗ⁾ + (1-β₁) ∇L(θ⁽ᵗ⁾)
2. s⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾ ← β₂s⁽ᵗ⁾ + (1-β₂) ∇L(θ⁽ᵗ⁾) ⊙ ∇L(θ⁽ᵗ⁾)
3. m̂⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾ ← m⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾ / (1 - β₁ᵗ)
4. ŝ⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾ ← s⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾ / (1 - β₂ᵗ)
5. θ⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾ ← θ⁽ᵗ⁾ - η m̂⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾ ⊘ (√ŝ⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾ + ε) - ηλθ⁽ᵗ⁾

Steps 1-4 are untouched Adam on the unregularized loss; only step 5 changed. And now it's trivial to see AdamW has the weight decay effect: the -ηλθ⁽ᵗ⁾ term means every parameter gets multiplied by (1 - ηλ) each step, exactly like in the SGD derivation above -- no √ŝ in sight to cancel it. Large weights shrink at the rate you asked for, full stop. This is why it's called decoupled weight decay.

One caveat worth knowing: because the decay bypasses the loss entirely, AdamW is no longer minimizing L*(θ) = L(θ) + (λ/2)‖θ‖² in any strict sense -- it's Adam on L plus a separate shrinkage step bolted on. The two are only equivalent for SGD. It's a pragmatic fix that happens to work extremely well in practice, not a principled derivation from a single objective. (A related practical note: the weight_decay argument in torch.optim.Adam implements the broken L2-in-gradient version, while torch.optim.AdamW implements the decoupled one -- so the fix is genuinely a one-argument change away.)

Summary

  • In SGD, adding L2 loss gives θ ← (1-ηλ)θ - η∇L(θ) -- L2 regularization and weight decay are the same thing.
  • In Adam, the λθ term goes through the second-moment scaling, which shrinks precisely the large gradients the regularizer created -- so the weight decay effect quietly disappears.
  • AdamW keeps the moment estimates on the plain gradient and subtracts λθ directly in the parameter update, restoring true weight decay.
  • The whole fix is one term moved from step 2 to step 5. That's it.

My Take

What I love about this note is how small the actual insight is. Loshchilov and Hutter didn't invent a new optimizer -- they noticed that an identity everyone took for granted ("L2 = weight decay") silently stops holding once you divide by √ŝ, and moved one term. That single moved term is now baked into essentially every LLM training run on the planet. It's a good reminder that "equivalent" always comes with an asterisk: equivalent under which update rule? Whenever I compose two techniques that were each derived in isolation (a regularizer here, an adaptive optimizer there), I now try to actually write out the combined update once, by hand. It's five lines of algebra, and it's exactly how you catch this kind of bug -- the kind that never throws an error, just quietly does less than you think.
