Conditional and Style GAN

Generative AI

A concise handwritten version of this note is available as a PDF.

Once a GAN is trained, we can sample z ~ N(0, I), pass it through the generator, and get a high quality image. Great — except we cannot control what image we get. Suppose we trained on images of animals and now we want a dog. Our only option is to keep sampling z over and over until one of the outputs happens to be a dog. That's embarrassingly inefficient, and it's the problem this whole note is about.

                                        x_i (real image)
                                            \
z_i --> [ g(z; θ) ] --> x*_i  --------> [ f(x; φ) ] --> P(x is real)
 |                    (generated image,
latent variable,       cannot control it)
~ N(0, I)

Why Can't Each Dimension of z Control One Thing?

A natural question I had: in a standard GAN, why doesn't each feature of z control one part of the image? Say z₁ for color, z₂ for background, and so on. Then controlling generation would be trivial.

Here's the argument for why this can't be true: if each dimension independently controlled one attribute, then all possible combinations of attributes would be equally likely — and many combinations violate how the real world works. A concrete example: suppose z₁ controls gender and z₂ controls beard, independently. Then "male + beard" and "female + beard" would be generated with equal probability. The second one is much less realistic, so a generator that produces realistic images simply cannot let those two dimensions act independently. The features have to be entangled.

The way I think about it: the latent variable z encodes all the information for generating the image, and the generator acts as a decoder. It decodes z into an image without any disentanglement of features.

One nice property survives, though: the latent space in a GAN is smooth. Interpolate between two latent codes and the image morphs smoothly:

z₁ --> generator --> image A
 |
 z' --> generator --> smooth change from A to B   (latent space interpolation)
 |
z₂ --> generator --> image B

But because features are highly entangled, changing one component of z changes many parts of the image at once. Smooth, yes. Controllable, no.

Conditional GAN: Just Tell It What You Want

The most direct fix is to pass the desired attribute in explicitly. Alongside z_i, we feed the generator an attribute vector c_i — e.g. a class embedding. If we want a cat, we pass the embedding for the cat category.

z_i, c_i --> [ g(z, c; θ) ] --> (x*_i, c_i) --> [ f(x, c; φ) ] --> P(pair (x, c) is real)
                                                     ^
                                       real pairs (x_j, c_j) also fed in:
                                       a real image of a cat always comes
                                       with the attribute vector for cat

The key change is on the discriminator side: it no longer judges the image alone, it judges the pair (x, c). A realistic image of a dog paired with the attribute vector for cat comes from a different joint distribution than real pairs — so the discriminator can catch it. Now the generator has to produce an image that is not just realistic, but realistic and consistent with the given attribute.

Auxiliary Classifier GAN

A variant on the same idea. The generator still takes (z_i, c_i) with c_i a class embedding, but the discriminator now has two heads: P(x is real) and P(x belongs to each class). The discriminator learns to minimize L_adversarial + L_classification, while the generator learns to minimize -L_adversarial + L_classification — i.e. fool the realness head, but cooperate with the classification head so the generated image is recognizably the right class.

InfoGAN: Make the Model Discover the Attributes

Conditional GAN needs labeled attributes. InfoGAN asks: what if we don't have labels? We concatenate (z_i, c_i) where c_i is drawn randomly — it could come from:

  • a categorical distribution, where each category represents a class;
  • a list of class embeddings;
  • a uniform distribution U[0, 1] — this could end up controlling, say, rotation angle.
(z_i, c_i) --> [ g(z, c; θ) ] --> x*_i --> [ f(x; φ) ] --> P(x is real)
                                                       --> estimate c_i  (reconstruct c)

The discriminator gets a second job: reconstruct the random vector c from the generated image, and both the generator and discriminator minimize this reconstruction loss. This is the clever part: because c needs to be reconstructed, the generator is forced to encode important information into the image so that c can be recovered from it — things like class, style, posture. Meanwhile z ends up deciding the unimportant features. Nobody told the model what c should mean; the training objective made it mean something.

Style GAN: Restructure the Generator Itself

StyleGAN goes further and redesigns the generator around controllability. Three ingredients:

            noise 4x4x1        noise 4x4x1        noise 8x8x1
            x learnable        x learnable        x learnable
            scaling (1x1x512)  scaling            scaling
                 |                  |                  |
trainable        v                  v                  v
constant --> [4x4x512] --------> [4x4x512] ------> [8x8x512] --> ... --> image
input            ^                  ^                  ^
                 | scale+offset     | scale+offset     | scale+offset
                 | per channel      | per channel      | per channel
              (1x1x1024)         (1x1x1024)         (1x1x1024)
                 |                  |                  |
              wM₁+b₁             wM₂+b₂             wM₃+b₃   (linear transforms)
                 \__________________|__________________/
                                    |
        z ~ N(0,I) --> dense layers (e.g. 7) --> w (1x1x512, "intermediate variable")
  • Style: the latent z ~ N(0, I) is first mapped through a stack of dense layers (e.g. 7) into an intermediate variable w. At every resolution level, a linear transform wM + b turns w into per-channel scale and offset values that modulate the feature maps.
  • Noise: at each level, spatial noise (e.g. 4x4x1) is added per channel, multiplied by a learnable scaling factor (1x1x512).
  • Constant input: the pipeline doesn't even start from z — it starts from a trainable constant tensor. All the variation comes in through w and the noise.

Why Is the Noise Scaling 4x4x1 Times a Learnable 1x1x512, Not Just 4x4x512?

This detail bugged me until I saw the insight: each channel may control one detail of the image — darkness, pores, hairs, etc. All spatial positions within a channel need a consistent signal to work together and make a realistic combination — which is why the noise itself is 4x4x1, shared across channels spatially. But different features may need different levels of noise (hair needs more randomness than overall darkness, say), so we give each channel its own learnable scaling factor.

Why Map z to w First?

z is Gaussian noise, and as we argued at the start, it is hard for the model to use one dimension of a Gaussian to control one feature — everything is entangled. The mapping network lets the model learn a new distribution for w that fits well with real world images, and in doing so it can untangle the features.

After training, we can actually edit w to control the output. How do we find, say, the component of w that controls gender? Empirically:

z₁ ... zₙ --> generator --> images of male    --> w₁ ... wₙ
                        --> images of female  --> w*₁ ... w*ₙ

gender vector  w̃ = w̄ - w̄*     (difference of averages)

w        --> generator --> an image of a female
w + w̃   --> generator --> an image of a male     (operation in latent space)

It is quite surprising to me that this simple vector arithmetic in latent space actually works — average the w's of male images, subtract the average of female images, and you get a direction you can add to any w to change gender.

Why Would Style GAN Work?

There's an implicit assumption underneath all of this, and I find it a genuinely interesting claim about neural networks: if there are many ways for a neural network to solve a problem, it has a bias to pick the easy one. The architecture makes the disentangled solution the easy one — modulate global attributes through w, sprinkle randomness through per-layer noise — so that's what training converges to. It's an inductive-bias argument, not a proof; but it holds up remarkably well in practice.

The division of labor that emerges:

  • Intermediate variable w (global): it controls every layer, so a change of any part of w affects the whole image — gender, pose, identity.
  • Per-layer noise (local): it controls details that make an image look more realistic but don't matter that much — e.g. the exact position of hairs. A change of random noise plays out at the pixel level.

My Take

What ties this note together for me is the entanglement argument at the beginning. Once you accept that a realistic generator cannot have independently meaningful latent dimensions (because independent dimensions imply all attribute combinations are equally likely, including physically implausible ones), the three fixes each make sense as a different bargain: Conditional GAN buys control with labels, InfoGAN manufactures control by forcing a code to be reconstructable, and StyleGAN restructures the generator so that disentanglement becomes the path of least resistance.

StyleGAN's "networks pick the easy solution" assumption is the part I keep coming back to. It's hand-wavy, unproven, and it works — which is a pretty accurate summary of a lot of deep learning. The caveat is that none of these methods gives you guaranteed disentanglement; you still have to go hunting in latent space (like the gender-vector trick) to find out what the model actually learned.
