Diffusion Models

Generative AI

A concise handwritten version of this note is available as a PDF.

A diffusion model consists of an encoder and a decoder. The encoder takes an image and gradually adds noise to it until the image becomes noise. The decoder reverses this process until it re-creates the image. What training the decoder actually reduces to is a strikingly simple objective: predict the noise that was added to an image. This note works through the derivation of why that's the objective.

Encoder and Decoder

Drawn out, the process looks like this:

              ------------- encode ------------->

  an image  --add noise-->  noisy image  --add noise--> ... --> Gaussian noise
            <--denoise---                <--denoise---  ...

              <------------ decode --------------

The encoder in diffusion is fixed and simple. At each step:

$$\begin{aligned} z_1 &= \sqrt{1-\beta_1}\, x + \sqrt{\beta_1}\, \epsilon_1 \\ z_t &= \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\, z_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}\, \epsilon_t , \qquad \text{where } \epsilon_1, ..., \epsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) \text{ i.i.d.} \end{aligned}$$

Here $\beta_t$ is the noise schedule — how much noise we inject at step $t$. Whatever the distribution of $x$ looks like (and image distributions are wildly multi-modal), after enough steps everything gets washed into a standard Gaussian.

A Property That Is Not Immediately Obvious

The encoder looks easy. But one property of it is not immediately clear: we can jump to any step in one shot. Unrolling two steps,

$$\begin{aligned} z_t &= \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\, z_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}\, \epsilon_t \\ &= \sqrt{1-\beta_t} \left( \sqrt{1-\beta_{t-1}}\, x + \sqrt{\beta_{t-1}}\, \epsilon_{t-1} \right) + \sqrt{\beta_t}\, \epsilon_t \\ &= \sqrt{(1-\beta_t)(1-\beta_{t-1})}\, x + (\text{noise term}) , \qquad \text{noise} \sim \mathcal{N}\big(0, (1-(1-\beta_t)(1-\beta_{t-1})) I\big) \end{aligned}$$

The two Gaussian noises merge into one (sum of independent Gaussians is Gaussian — the variances just add up). Repeating this all the way down:

$$p(z_t \mid x) = \mathcal{N}\big( \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x ,\, (1-\bar\alpha_t) I \big) , \qquad \text{i.e.} \quad z_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\, \epsilon$$ $$\text{where} \quad \bar\alpha_t = \prod_{i=1}^{t} (1-\beta_i)$$

This is the identity that makes training practical later — we never have to simulate the chain step by step to get a noisy sample.

The Decoder, and Why We Condition on $x$

Our goal is to learn the true reverse distribution $p(z_{t-1} \mid z_t)$. Unfortunately it has no closed form: by Bayes it involves $p(z_{t-1}) = \int p(z_{t-1}|x)\, p(x)\, dx$, and $p(x)$ is the distribution of natural images — exactly the thing we don't know.

However — and this is very important — $p(z_{t-1} \mid z_t, x)$ does have closed form. Conditioning on the clean image makes everything Gaussian, and standard Gaussian identities give:

$$p(z_{t-1} \mid z_t, x) = \mathcal{N}\left( \frac{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1-\bar\alpha_t} \cdot \sqrt{1-\beta_t} \cdot z_t + \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}} \cdot \frac{\beta_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t} \cdot x ,\; \frac{\beta_t (1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t} \cdot I \right)$$

This distribution is what we will learn to imitate for reversing. The decoder approximates the reverse chain with Gaussians:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{pr}(z_T) &= \mathcal{N}(0, I) \\ \mathrm{pr}(z_{t-1} \mid z_t) &= \mathcal{N}\big( f_t(z_t; \phi_t) ,\, \sigma_t^2 I \big) \qquad \leftarrow \text{the neural network lives in } f_t \\ \mathrm{pr}(x \mid z_1) &= \mathcal{N}\big( f_1(z_1; \phi_1) ,\, \sigma_1^2 I \big) \end{aligned}$$

Two things worth noting: the larger the number of steps $T$, the better this Gaussian approximation becomes (each reverse step only needs to undo a tiny bit of noise); and $\sigma_t^2$ is pre-determined, e.g. 1 or 0.5 — we don't learn it.

Training: ELBO, and Why Diffusion Beats VAE Here

Ideally we'd maximize the marginal likelihood $\mathrm{pr}(x \mid \phi_{1..T})$, but that integral over all $z_1, ..., z_T$ is intractable. So, as in VAE, we work with its lower bound (ELBO):

$$\log \mathrm{pr}(x) \ge \int q(z_1..z_T \mid x) \log \left[ \frac{\mathrm{pr}(x, z_1..z_T \mid \phi)}{q(z_1..z_T \mid x)} \right] dz = \mathrm{ELBO}$$

where $q$ is the encoder distribution. The gap between ELBO and the true log-likelihood is exactly $\mathrm{KL}\big(q(z_{1..T} \mid x) \,\|\, \mathrm{pr}(z_{1..T} \mid x, \phi)\big)$.

This is where diffusion has a structural advantage over VAE. In VAE, both encoder and decoder optimize the ELBO, and the encoder can help close the gap — but a Gaussian variational encoder may be far from the true posterior, so the ELBO is often not close to the marginal likelihood, which is one reason VAE images look blurry. In diffusion, the encoder is fixed for a given $x$, so the decoder has to do all the work — and crucially, the thing the decoder must match, $q(z_{t-1} \mid z_t, x)$, is a fixed Gaussian. Much easier to learn than chasing a moving target that depends on the decoder itself.

Simplify the ELBO

Expanding the joint in the reverse direction and the $q$ in the forward direction, then applying Bayes' theorem to flip $q(z_t \mid z_{t-1})$ into $q(z_{t-1} \mid z_t, x)$ (this flip is the crucial move — it aligns each $q$ term with a decoder term going the same direction), the ELBO becomes:

$$\mathrm{ELBO} \approx \mathbb{E}\big[ \log \mathrm{pr}(x \mid z_1, \phi_1) \big] - \sum_{t=2}^{T} \mathbb{E}\Big[ \mathrm{KL}\big( q(z_{t-1} \mid z_t, x) \,\|\, \mathrm{pr}(z_{t-1} \mid z_t, \phi_t) \big) \Big]$$

(The leftover term $\log\big(\mathrm{pr}(z_T)/q(z_T|x)\big)$ is $\approx 0$, since after $T$ steps $q(z_T|x)$ is very close to standard normal.) The first term is a reconstruction error; each KL term compares two Gaussians, which has an explicit form — up to constants, it is just the squared distance between the two means:

$$\text{KL term} \propto \frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2} \Big\| \text{mean of } q(z_{t-1} \mid z_t, x) - f_t(z_t; \phi_t) \Big\|^2$$

We could stop here. This loss can already train a network to reverse the process: at each step, learn to predict the mean of $q(z_{t-1} \mid z_t, x)$. But there is a nicer form.

Reparameterization: It All Collapses to Predicting Noise

Recall $z_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\, \epsilon$, so $x = z_t/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} - \frac{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}} \cdot \epsilon$. Substituting this into the mean of $q(z_{t-1} \mid z_t, x)$ and grinding through the algebra (all the $\bar\alpha$ factors cancel), the target mean becomes:

$$\text{mean of } q(z_{t-1} \mid z_t, x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\beta_t}} \cdot z_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\sqrt{1-\beta_t}} \cdot \epsilon$$

So instead of predicting the mean directly, we parameterize the network in exactly the same shape and let it predict $\epsilon$:

$$f_t(z_t; \phi_t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\beta_t}} \cdot z_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\sqrt{1-\beta_t}} \cdot g_t(z_t; \phi_t)$$

The difference of means is then just the difference of noise terms, and the whole loss collapses to:

$$L(\phi_{1..T}) \propto \sum_i \sum_t \frac{\beta_t^2}{2\sigma_t^2 (1-\bar\alpha_t)(1-\beta_t)} \cdot \Big\| \epsilon_{it} - g_t\big( \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_i + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\, \epsilon_{it} ;\, \phi_t \big) \Big\|^2$$

Noise up an image to step $t$, ask the network which noise was used, penalize the squared error. That's the entire training objective.

Why Is This Better Than the Loss Before Reparameterization?

Although mathematically equivalent, the new loss is much more practical because it decomposes into a sum over sample size and step size. Before, to train with SGD we'd sample $b$ images and train all $T$ steps for each — if $T$ is large, $b$ has to be tiny (or doesn't fit in RAM at all), and with $b=1, T=1000$ the losses within a batch are highly correlated while gradient directions across batches vary wildly. Now we simply sample $B$ pairs from all combinations of image and step — a proper i.i.d.-ish minibatch.

Note that the $\epsilon$'s we regress against at different steps do not come from independent noise (they're all tied to the same $z_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\, \epsilon$ decomposition). So why would learning the noise work? Because on average, the noise points in the right direction to move from the target distribution toward the standard normal — and its negation points back.

Sampling: Don't Forget to Add Noise Back

Generation runs the learned chain backwards:

$$\begin{aligned} &z_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) \\ &\text{for } t = T, ..., 2: \\ &\qquad \hat{z}_{t-1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\beta_t}} \cdot z_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\sqrt{1-\beta_t}} \cdot g_t(z_t; \phi_t) \\ &\qquad z_{t-1} = \hat{z}_{t-1} + \sigma_t \epsilon , \qquad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) \\ &x = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\beta_1}} \cdot z_1 - \frac{\beta_1}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_1}\sqrt{1-\beta_1}} \cdot g_1(z_1; \phi_1) \end{aligned}$$

The subtle point: $\hat{z}_{t-1}$ is only the mean of the reverse distribution — the "average" direction at the current step. In image generation, average means blurry (the average of many dog images is a grey smudge, not a dog). It is important to add noise back at each step — by adding noise, we force the image to add details. Strictly speaking noise should be added at the very last step too, but in practice it doesn't matter.

Summary

  • The encoder is fixed: gradually Gaussian-noise an image, with the one-shot identity $z_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\, \epsilon$.
  • $p(z_{t-1} \mid z_t)$ is intractable, but conditioning on $x$ makes it a closed-form Gaussian — that's the target the decoder learns.
  • The ELBO reduces to per-step Gaussian KLs, and because the encoder is fixed, the decoder chases a fixed target — the key advantage over VAE.
  • Reparameterization turns everything into noise prediction, which decomposes the loss over (image, step) pairs and plays nicely with SGD.
  • At sampling time, add noise back at every step — the mean alone gives you blurry averages, the noise gives you details.
