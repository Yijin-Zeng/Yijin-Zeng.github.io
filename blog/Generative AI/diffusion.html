Diffusion Models

Generative AI

A concise handwritten version of this note is available as a PDF.

Every image generator you've played with recently — the ones behind those photorealistic pictures of astronauts riding horses — starts from pure Gaussian noise and gradually sculpts it into an image. It is quite surprising to me when I first know that the model behind this is trained to do something that sounds almost trivial: predict the noise that was added to an image. That's it. This note works through why that simple objective falls out of a fairly long (but satisfying) derivation.

Encoder and Decoder

A diffusion model consists of an encoder and a decoder. The encoder takes an image and gradually adds noise to it until the image becomes noise. The decoder reverses this process until it re-creates the image.

              ------------- encode ------------->

  an image  --add noise-->  noisy image  --add noise--> ... --> Gaussian noise
            <--denoise---                <--denoise---  ...

              <------------ decode --------------

The encoder in diffusion is fixed and simple. At each step:

z_1 = √(1-β_1) x     + √β_1 ε_1
z_t = √(1-β_t) z_t-1 + √β_t ε_t ,     where ε_1, ..., ε_t ~ N(0, I) i.i.d.

Here β_t is the noise schedule — how much noise we inject at step t. Whatever the distribution of x looks like (and image distributions are wildly multi-modal), after enough steps everything gets washed into a standard Gaussian.

A Property That Is Not Immediately Obvious

The encoder looks easy. But one property of it is not immediately clear: we can jump to any step in one shot. Unrolling two steps,

z_t = √(1-β_t) z_t-1 + √β_t ε_t
    = √(1-β_t) (√(1-β_t-1) x + √β_t-1 ε_t-1) + √β_t ε_t
    = √((1-β_t)(1-β_t-1)) x + (noise term) ,   noise ~ N(0, (1-(1-β_t)(1-β_t-1)) I)

The two Gaussian noises merge into one (sum of independent Gaussians is Gaussian — the variances just add up). Repeating this all the way down:

p(z_t | x) = N( √ᾱ_t x , (1-ᾱ_t) I ) ,   i.e.  z_t = √ᾱ_t x + √(1-ᾱ_t) ε

where  ᾱ_t = ∏_{i=1}^{t} (1-β_i)

This is the identity that makes training practical later — we never have to simulate the chain step by step to get a noisy sample.

The Decoder, and Why We Condition on x

Our goal is to learn the true reverse distribution p(z_t-1 | z_t). Unfortunately it has no closed form: by Bayes it involves p(z_t-1) = ∫ p(z_t-1|x) p(x) dx, and p(x) is the distribution of natural images — exactly the thing we don't know.

However — and this is very important — p(z_t-1 | z_t, x) does have closed form. Conditioning on the clean image makes everything Gaussian, and standard Gaussian identities give:

p(z_t-1 | z_t, x) = N( (1-ᾱ_t-1)/(1-ᾱ_t) · √(1-β_t) · z_t
                       + √ᾱ_t-1 · β_t/(1-ᾱ_t) · x ,
                       β_t (1-ᾱ_t-1)/(1-ᾱ_t) · I )

This distribution is what we will learn to imitate for reversing. The decoder approximates the reverse chain with Gaussians:

pr(z_T)        = N(0, I)
pr(z_t-1 | z_t) = N( f_t(z_t; φ_t) , σ_t² I )      <- the neural network lives in f_t
pr(x | z_1)     = N( f_1(z_1; φ_1) , σ_1² I )

Two notes from the margin of my handwritten version: the larger the number of steps T, the better this Gaussian approximation becomes (each reverse step only needs to undo a tiny bit of noise); and σ_t² is pre-determined, e.g. 1 or 0.5 — we don't learn it.

Training: ELBO, and Why Diffusion Beats VAE Here

Ideally we'd maximize the marginal likelihood pr(x | φ_1..T), but that integral over all z_1, ..., z_T is intractable. So, as in VAE, we work with its lower bound (ELBO):

log pr(x) ≥ ∫ q(z_1..z_T | x) log [ pr(x, z_1..z_T | φ) / q(z_1..z_T | x) ] dz  =  ELBO

where q is the encoder distribution. The gap between ELBO and the true log-likelihood is exactly KL(q(z_1..T | x) || pr(z_1..T | x, φ)).

Here is the part I find genuinely insightful. In VAE, both encoder and decoder optimize the ELBO, and the encoder can help close the gap — but a Gaussian variational encoder may be far from the true posterior, so the ELBO is often not close to the marginal likelihood, which is one reason VAE images look blurry. In diffusion, the encoder is fixed for a given x, so the decoder has to do all the work — and crucially, the thing the decoder must match, q(z_t-1 | z_t, x), is a fixed Gaussian. Much easier to learn than chasing a moving target that depends on the decoder itself.

Simplify the ELBO

Expanding the joint in the reverse direction and the q in the forward direction, then applying Bayes' theorem to flip q(z_t | z_t-1) into q(z_t-1 | z_t, x) (this flip is the crucial move — it aligns each q term with a decoder term going the same direction), the ELBO becomes:

ELBO ≈ E[ log pr(x | z_1, φ_1) ]  -  Σ_{t=2}^{T} E[ KL( q(z_t-1 | z_t, x) || pr(z_t-1 | z_t, φ_t) ) ]

(The leftover term log(pr(z_T)/q(z_T|x)) is ≈ 0, since after T steps q(z_T|x) is very close to standard normal.) The first term is a reconstruction error; each KL term compares two Gaussians, which has an explicit form — up to constants, it is just the squared distance between the two means:

KL term ∝ (1/2σ_t²) || mean of q(z_t-1 | z_t, x)  -  f_t(z_t; φ_t) ||²

We could stop here. This loss can already train a network to reverse the process: at each step, learn to predict the mean of q(z_t-1 | z_t, x). But there is a nicer form.

Reparameterization: It All Collapses to Predicting Noise

Recall z_t = √ᾱ_t x + √(1-ᾱ_t) ε, so x = z_t/√ᾱ_t - √(1-ᾱ_t)/√ᾱ_t · ε. Substituting this into the mean of q(z_t-1 | z_t, x) and grinding through the algebra (about a page of it in the PDF, all the ᾱ factors cancelling beautifully), the target mean becomes:

mean of q(z_t-1 | z_t, x)  =  1/√(1-β_t) · z_t  -  β_t/(√(1-ᾱ_t)√(1-β_t)) · ε

So instead of predicting the mean directly, we parameterize the network in exactly the same shape and let it predict ε:

f_t(z_t; φ_t)  =  1/√(1-β_t) · z_t  -  β_t/(√(1-ᾱ_t)√(1-β_t)) · g_t(z_t; φ_t)

The difference of means is then just the difference of noise terms, and the whole loss collapses to:

L(φ_1..T) ∝ Σ_i Σ_t  β_t²/(2σ_t²(1-ᾱ_t)(1-β_t)) · || ε_it - g_t( √ᾱ_t x_i + √(1-ᾱ_t) ε_it ; φ_t ) ||²

Noise up an image to step t, ask the network which noise was used, penalize the squared error. That's the entire training objective.

Why Is This Better Than the Loss Before Reparameterization?

Although mathematically equivalent, the new loss is much more practical because it decomposes into a sum over sample size and step size. Before, to train with SGD we'd sample b images and train all T steps for each — if T is large, b has to be tiny (or doesn't fit in RAM at all), and with b=1, T=1000 the losses within a batch are highly correlated while gradient directions across batches vary wildly. Now we simply sample B pairs from all combinations of image and step — a proper i.i.d.-ish minibatch.

One thing that puzzled me: the ε's we regress against at different steps do not come from independent noise (they're all tied to the same z_t = √ᾱ_t x + √(1-ᾱ_t) ε decomposition). So why would learning the noise work? Because on average, the noise points in the right direction to move from the target distribution toward the standard normal — and its negation points back.

Sampling: Don't Forget to Add Noise Back

Generation runs the learned chain backwards:

z_T ~ N(0, I)
for t = T, ..., 2:
    ẑ_t-1 = 1/√(1-β_t) · z_t - β_t/(√(1-ᾱ_t)√(1-β_t)) · g_t(z_t; φ_t)
    z_t-1 = ẑ_t-1 + σ_t ε ,    ε ~ N(0, I)
x = 1/√(1-β_1) · z_1 - β_1/(√(1-ᾱ_1)√(1-β_1)) · g_1(z_1; φ_1)

The subtle point: ẑ_t-1 is only the mean of the reverse distribution — the "average" direction at the current step. In image generation, average means blurry (the average of many dog images is a grey smudge, not a dog). It is important to add noise back at each step — by adding noise, we force the image to add details. Strictly speaking noise should be added at the very last step too, but in practice it doesn't matter.

Summary

  • The encoder is fixed: gradually Gaussian-noise an image, with the one-shot identity z_t = √ᾱ_t x + √(1-ᾱ_t) ε.
  • p(z_t-1 | z_t) is intractable, but conditioning on x makes it a closed-form Gaussian — that's the target the decoder learns.
  • The ELBO reduces to per-step Gaussian KLs, and because the encoder is fixed, the decoder chases a fixed target — the key advantage over VAE.
  • Reparameterization turns everything into noise prediction, which decomposes the loss over (image, step) pairs and plays nicely with SGD.
  • At sampling time, add noise back at every step — the mean alone gives you blurry averages, the noise gives you details.

My Take

What sticks with me from working through this derivation is how much of diffusion's success comes down to choosing which distribution to learn. The true reverse p(z_t-1 | z_t) is hopeless; the conditioned one p(z_t-1 | z_t, x) is a plain Gaussian. One Bayes flip and everything downstream — the ELBO simplification, the noise-prediction loss, the SGD-friendly decomposition — follows almost mechanically. Compared to GANs (no adversarial instability) and VAEs (no encoder eating the ELBO gap), diffusion trades elegance of a single-shot generator for a long chain of very easy problems.

The cost, of course, is that the chain is long: sampling requires hundreds or thousands of network evaluations, one per step. That's the price of making each step trivially Gaussian — and it's exactly the gap that later work on faster samplers and distillation tries to close.
