Nonuniform Random Number Generation

Mathematics

A concise handwritten version of this note is available as a PDF.

Most random number generators only give us samples from $\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$. The question we look at here is how to turn those uniform samples into samples from some other distribution $F(x)$.

Inversion Method

Let $U \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$, and let $X \sim F(x)$ where $F^{-1}(\cdot)$ exists. Then

$$F^{-1}(U) \sim F(x)$$

Proof:

$$P(F^{-1}(U) \le x) = P(U \le F(x)) = F(x)$$

So if we can compute the inverse CDF, we can plug a uniform sample straight into it and get a sample from the target distribution.

Example: generate the exponential distribution. We have $f_X(x) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}$. Integrating gives the CDF:

$$\begin{aligned} F(x) &= \lambda \int_0^x e^{-\lambda z}\, dz \\ &= \lambda \left( -\frac{1}{\lambda} e^{-\lambda z} \Big|_{z=0}^{x} \right) \\ &= \lambda \left( \frac{1}{\lambda} - \frac{1}{\lambda} e^{-\lambda x} \right) \\ &= 1 - e^{-\lambda x} \end{aligned}$$

Let $y = 1 - \exp(-\lambda x)$. Solving for $x$:

$$\begin{aligned} \exp(-\lambda x) &= 1 - y \\ \Rightarrow\; -\lambda x &= \log(1-y) \\ \Rightarrow\; x &= \frac{\log(1-y)}{-\lambda} \end{aligned}$$

so $F^{-1}(x) = \log(1-x) / (-\lambda)$. Combined with the inversion result above,

$$\frac{\log(1-U)}{-\lambda} \sim \mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)$$

The problem with this method is that not all distributions have $F^{-1}(\cdot)$ in closed form. For example, the Gaussian distribution has no explicit form for $F^{-1}(\cdot)$.

Acceptance-Rejection Method

Suppose we have a density function $f$ we cannot directly sample from, but we can sample from another density function $g$, such that there exists a constant $C < \infty$ with

$$f(x) \le C g(x), \quad \forall x$$

Then the acceptance-rejection method does:

While True:
    X ~ g(x)
    U ~ Uniform(0,1)
    if U ≤ f(X) / (Cg(X)):
        accept X

Proof that the accepted samples follow $f$:

$$\begin{aligned} P\!\left(X \le z \,\Big|\, U \le \frac{f(X)}{Cg(X)}\right) &= \frac{P\!\left(X \le z,\, U \le \frac{f(X)}{Cg(X)}\right)}{P\!\left(U \le \frac{f(X)}{Cg(X)}\right)} \\ &= \frac{\int_{-\infty}^{z} \int_0^{f(x)/(Cg(x))} g(x)\, du\, dx}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \int_0^{f(x)/(Cg(x))} g(x)\, du\, dx} \\ &= \frac{\int_{-\infty}^{z} f(x)/C\, dx}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)/C\, dx} \\ &= \int_{-\infty}^{z} f(x)\, dx \\ &= F_X(z) \end{aligned}$$

The inner integral over $U$ just picks up the height $f(x)/(Cg(x))$, which cancels one factor of $g(x)$ and leaves $f(x)/C$. The $C$ in the denominator then cancels against the $C$ in the numerator, leaving exactly the CDF of $f$.

Box-Muller Method

Let $\theta \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0, 2\pi)$ and $R^2 \sim \mathrm{Exp}(1/2)$. If $R^2 \sim \mathrm{Exp}(1/2)$, then

$$\begin{aligned} P(R < r) &= P(R^2 < r^2) \\ &= \int_0^{r^2} \frac{1}{2} e^{-z/2}\, dz \\ &= 1 - e^{-r^2/2} \end{aligned}$$

Differentiating gives the density of $R$:

$$f_R(r) = \left(-e^{-r^2/2}\right)' = r e^{-r^2/2}$$

Now let $X = R \cos\theta$, $Y = R \sin\theta$. The claim is that $X, Y \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$, independently.

Proof: Denote the map $f((R,\theta)) = (X,Y)$. Since $X = R\cos\theta$, $Y = R\sin\theta$, we have

$$X^2 + Y^2 = R^2 \;\Rightarrow\; R = \sqrt{X^2+Y^2} = (X^2+Y^2)^{1/2}$$

Also, $Y/X = \tan\theta$, so $\theta = \tan^{-1}(Y/X)$. So $f^{-1}$ exists, and the Jacobian of $(R,\theta)$ with respect to $(x,y)$ is

$$J = \begin{bmatrix} x(x^2+y^2)^{-1/2} & y(x^2+y^2)^{-1/2} \\[4pt] -\dfrac{1}{1+(y/x)^2} \cdot \dfrac{y}{x^2} & \dfrac{1}{1+(y/x)^2} \cdot \dfrac{1}{x} \end{bmatrix}$$

Taking the determinant:

$$\begin{aligned} |J| &= x(x^2+y^2)^{-1/2} \cdot \frac{1}{1+(y/x)^2} \cdot \frac{1}{x} + y(x^2+y^2)^{-1/2} \cdot \frac{1}{1+(y/x)^2} \cdot \frac{y}{x^2} \\ &= (x^2+y^2)^{-1/2} \cdot \frac{1}{1+(y/x)^2} \cdot \left(1 + \frac{y^2}{x^2}\right) \\ &= (x^2+y^2)^{-1/2} \\ &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}} \end{aligned}$$

So the joint density of $X, Y$ is

$$\begin{aligned} f_{X,Y}(x,y) &= f_R(r) \cdot f_\theta(\theta) \cdot |J| \\ &= \sqrt{x^2+y^2} \cdot \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}(x^2+y^2)\right) \cdot \frac{1}{2\pi} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}} \\ &= \frac{1}{2\pi} \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}(x^2+y^2)\right) \\ &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{x^2}{2}\right) \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{y^2}{2}\right) \end{aligned}$$

which factors into the product of two independent standard normal densities. So drawing $\theta \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,2\pi)$ and $R^2 \sim \mathrm{Exp}(1/2)$ and setting $X = R\cos\theta$, $Y = R\sin\theta$ gives two independent samples from $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$, without ever needing the Gaussian inverse CDF.

(Box & Muller, 1958.)
