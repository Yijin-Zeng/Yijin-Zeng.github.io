Constrained Optimization

Mathematics

A concise handwritten version of this note is available as a PDF.

The Standard Form

We sometimes face constrained optimization problems:

$$\begin{aligned} (q)\quad & \min_{x} f(x) \\ & \text{s.t. } g_i(x) \le 0 \quad \text{for all } i = 1, \dots, m \end{aligned}$$

This form is known as the standard form for constrained optimization problem. Any other formulation can be converted into this. For example, if we want to maximize $f(x)$ rather than minimize it, we have $\max f(x) = \min -f(x)$. Also, an equality constraint $g(x) = 0$ can be equivalently written into two inequalities: $g(x) \le 0$ and $-g(x) \le 0$.

The Penalty Method (Naive Approach)

To solve the constrained problem as described above, a simple (but may be impractical) idea is to convert the problem into an unconstrained problem by adding a penalty for violating constraints:

$$\min_{x} \; f(x) + \sum_i I(g_i(x)), \qquad \text{where } I(z) = \begin{cases} 0 & z \le 0 \\ +\infty \text{ (or a large value if violating slightly is acceptable)} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

Why does the naive penalty method often not work well? It could make the loss space very bad. The infinite penalty creates walls in the loss surface; if gradient descent starts on the wrong side, it can never cross over. Even with a large finite penalty instead of infinity, the sharp discontinuity makes optimization extremely difficult.

Lagrange Multiplier Method

Instead of introducing penalty, let us consider how Lagrange multiplier method approach the constrained problem. First, define the Lagrangian form for the problem:

$$\begin{aligned} L(x, \lambda) &= f(x) + \lambda^\top g(x) \\[4pt] \text{where } \lambda &= (\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m)^\top \\ g(x) &= (g_1(x), \dots, g_m(x))^\top \end{aligned}$$

Notice that the original problem (q) is now equivalent to:

$$(p)\quad \min_{x} \max_{\lambda \ge 0} L(x, \lambda)$$

The reason is that for a feasible $x$ (where all $g_i(x) \le 0$), the max over $\lambda \ge 0$ just gives $f(x)$ since the $\lambda$ terms are non-positive. For an infeasible $x$, some $g_i(x) > 0$, and $\lambda$ can drive the Lagrangian to infinity. So the min-max formulation introduced by the Lagrangian form naturally enforces the constraints.

The Dual Problem

Next, we consider the so called dual problem of problem (p). First, notice that for any function $\phi(x, y)$, we have:

$$\begin{aligned} \min_x \phi(x,y) &\le \phi(x,y) \le \max_y \phi(x,y) \\ \Rightarrow\; \max_y \min_x \phi(x,y) &\le \min_x \max_y \phi(x,y) \end{aligned}$$

This gives us the Lagrange dual problem:

$$\begin{aligned} (d)\quad & \max_{\lambda} \min_{x} L(x, \lambda) \\ & \text{s.t. } \lambda \ge 0 \end{aligned}$$

Denote $q(\lambda) = \min_x L(x, \lambda)$ — this is called the inner problem. The dual problem in general much easier than the original problem (q). Consider the following three reasons:

  1. Simpler constraints: we go from $g_i(x) \le 0$ (which could be arbitrarily complex) to $\lambda_i \ge 0$ (simple box constraints, the easiest kind)
  2. Convexity: even if $\min f(x)$ is non-convex, $\max q(\lambda)$ is always a convex problem
  3. Dimensionality reduction (in some cases): say $x$ is in $\mathbb{R}^{10000}$ and we have 5 constraints; the dual reduces the problem to only 5 dimensions

Solve the Dual Problem

We now consider how to solving the dual problem (d).

  1. Ideal case: we solve the (unconstrained) inner problem, get the analytical form for the vector $x$. Then we put the form of $x$ back into the dual problem, solve the dual problem (by taking the gradient with respect to the vector $\lambda$ and setting the result to zero), find the solutions for each $\lambda_i \ge 0$, and we take the results as they are.
  2. Practical algorithm (dual ascent):
    1. Initialize: Start with an initial guess for the vector of penalty variables, $\lambda$, ensuring every individual penalty element starts at $\lambda_i \ge 0$.
    2. Primal Update (Inner Problem): Using the currently fixed $\lambda$ vector, computationally find the multi-dimensional vector $x$ that minimizes the Lagrangian: $x^{(k+1)} = \arg\min_x L(x, \lambda^k)$.
    3. Dual Update (Gradient Ascent): Evaluate the constraint violations for all $m$ constraints, $g_i(x^{(k+1)})$. Update each penalty variable in the $\lambda$ vector individually by taking a step in the direction of its specific gradient: $\lambda_i^{(k+1)} = \max(0, \lambda_i^k + \alpha \cdot g_i(x^{(k+1)}))$ (note that $g_i(x^{(k+1)})$ is the gradient wrt $\lambda_i$ at $x^{(k+1)}$) for all $i = 1, \dots, m$, where $\alpha$ is the step size. The $\max(0, \dots)$ operation ensures no individual penalty ever drops below zero.
    4. Iterate: Repeat steps (b) and (c) until the vectors $x$ and $\lambda$ both stabilize and stop changing (convergence).

Duality Gap

Although the dual problem could be much easier than the original problem. Keep in mind that the results for the dual problem could be smaller than the original problem. The difference between the original and dual solutions is called duality gap:

$$\begin{aligned} G &= q^* - d^* \\[4pt] \text{where } q^* &= \text{optimal value of original problem} \\ d^* &= \text{optimal value of dual problem} \end{aligned}$$
  • $G = 0$ (strong duality): the dual gives the exact solution
  • $G > 0$ (weak duality): the dual only gives a lower bound, which means the $x$ found by the dual is not in the feasible region of the original problem

When Does Strong Duality Hold?

Naturally, we want to know when the strong duality would hold. A sufficient (but not necessary) condition is Slater's condition:

  1. Convexity: $f(x)$ and all $g_i(x)$ are convex
  2. Strict feasibility: there exists at least one $x$ such that $g_i(x) < 0$ for all $i$ (strictly inside the feasible region, not on the boundary)

Why might weak duality happen? In the inner problem $\min_x (f(x) + \lambda^\top g(x))$, when $g_i(x) > 0$ (violating the constraint), we get a penalty $\lambda_i \cdot g_i(x)$. But if $f(x)$ is very small at that point, the violation might be worth it from the Lagrangian's perspective.

KKT Conditions

While the Lagrange method gives us a often simpler dual problem, it does not solve the problem directly. Let us first look at how the Lagrangian helps in two cases.

Case 1: Only Equality Constraints

$$\min_x f(x) \qquad \text{s.t. } g_i(x) = 0, \quad i = 1, \dots, m$$

An equality constraint can be written as two inequalities, $g_i(x) \ge 0$ and $g_i(x) \le 0$. So the Lagrange form is:

$$\begin{aligned} L(x, \lambda, \lambda') &= f(x) - \sum_{i=1}^{m} \lambda_i g_i(x) + \sum_{i=1}^{m} \lambda'_i g_i(x) \qquad \text{s.t. } \lambda_i \ge 0,\; \lambda'_i \ge 0 \\ \Leftrightarrow\quad L(x, \mu) &= f(x) - \sum_{i=1}^{m} \mu_i g_i(x) \end{aligned}$$

where $\mu_i = \lambda_i - \lambda'_i$ can take either sign. If we have only equality constraints, we get an unconstrained optimization problem. We solve

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L}{\partial x} &= 0 \qquad (n \text{ equations}) \\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mu_i} &= g_i(x) = 0 \qquad (m \text{ equations}) \end{aligned}$$

for the optimal $x$.

Case 2: With Inequality Constraints

$$\begin{aligned} &\min_x f(x) \\ &\text{s.t. } g_i(x) = 0, \quad h_i(x) \le 0 \end{aligned}$$

The Lagrange form becomes

$$L(x, \lambda, \mu) = f(x) + \sum_{i=1}^{m_1} \mu_i g_i(x) + \sum_{i=1}^{m_2} \lambda_i h_i(x) \qquad \text{s.t. } \lambda_i \ge 0$$
  1. If $h_i(x) < 0$ (constraint strictly satisfied), then in order to maximize $L(x, \lambda, \mu)$ over $\lambda_i$, we must have $\lambda_i = 0$.
  2. If $h_i(x) = 0$, then $\lambda_i \ge 0$.

Either way, we must have $\lambda_i h_i(x) = 0$!

The KKT Conditions

To solve constrained problems with inequality constraints, we often solve the constrained optimization problem directly using KKT (Karush-Kuhn-Tucker) conditions. These give a local minimum (or global, if Slater's condition holds).

The four conditions are:

  1. Stationarity: $\nabla_x f(x) + \lambda^\top \nabla_x g(x) = 0$ — at the minimum, the gradient of the Lagrangian must be zero
  2. Primal feasibility: $g_i(x) \le 0$ for all $i$ — the solution must satisfy the original constraints
  3. Dual feasibility: $\lambda_i \ge 0$ for all $i$ — multipliers must be non-negative
  4. Complementary slackness: $\lambda_i \cdot g_i(x) = 0$ for all $i$ — either the constraint is active ($g_i = 0$, and then $\lambda_i \ge 0$) or its multiplier is zero ($g_i < 0$, and then $\lambda_i = 0$)

The complementary slackness condition is the most interesting one. Intuitively, it says that if a constraint is not tight ($g_i(x) < 0$ we're strictly inside the feasible region), then $\lambda_i = 0$, i.e. that constraint doesn't matter at the optimum.

Both stationarity and complementary slackness come from the min-max form. Recall that the original problem is equivalent to

$$\min_x \max_{\lambda \ge 0} L(x, \lambda) = \min_x \max_{\lambda \ge 0} \big( f(x) + \lambda^\top g(x) \big)$$
  • Stationarity: if $x$ is a local minimum, then the gradient must be $0$ (for every $x_i$).
  • Complementary slackness: if $g_i(x) < 0$, then $\lambda_i = 0$, because for a fixed $x$, $\lambda$ maximizes $L(x, \lambda)$.

Using KKT to Find Solutions

KKT conditions are a checklist (a sufficient condition) for whether the $x^*, \lambda^*$ we have in hand are a valid local (or global, if Slater's condition holds) result. But they can also be used to find a local minimum. The idea is to construct $x^*, \lambda^*$ that satisfy the KKT conditions. The steps are:

  1. Guess which constraints are active (on the boundary); this follows complementary slackness condition which says for each constraint either $g_i(x) = 0$ or $\lambda_i = 0$
  2. Solve for $x$ and $\lambda$ from the stationarity and complementary slackness equations; suppose $x$ is in $\mathbb{R}^{k_1}$ and $\lambda$ is in $\mathbb{R}^{k_2}$, then stationarity codition provides $\mathbb{R}^{k_1}$ equations and complementary slackness provides $\mathbb{R}^{k_2}$ equations
  3. Check feasibility; if it passes, found a local minimum. If not, go back to step 1 with a different guess

Different guesses in step 1 can give different local minima. With $m$ constraints, there are $2^m$ possible combinations to try in the worst case. In some cases, the guess could be dramastically simpler. For example, if the target function is simply the sum of $x_i$, then we can evaluate if each $x_i$ is on the boundary seperately.

A Simple Example of a Constrained Problem

$$\begin{aligned} &\min_{\hat{p}_1, \dots, \hat{p}_K} \sum_{i=1}^{K} -p_i \log(\hat{p}_i) \\ &\text{s.t. } \sum_{i=1}^{K} \hat{p}_i = 1 \\ &\hphantom{\text{s.t. }} \hat{p}_i \ge 0 \quad \text{for all } i = 1, \dots, K \end{aligned}$$

The Lagrangian is

$$L(\hat{p}, \mu, \lambda) = -\sum_{i=1}^{K} p_i \log(\hat{p}_i) - \mu \Big( \sum_{i=1}^{K} \hat{p}_i - 1 \Big) - \sum_{i=1}^{K} \lambda_i \hat{p}_i$$

By complementary slackness, for the last term we need either $\lambda_i = 0$, or $\hat{p}_i = 0$. But $\hat{p}_i = 0$ would lead the objective to $\infty$, so we must have $\lambda_i = 0$, and the last term drops out:

$$L(\hat{p}, \mu) = -\sum_{i=1}^{K} p_i \log(\hat{p}_i) - \mu \Big( \sum_{i=1}^{K} \hat{p}_i - 1 \Big)$$

Taking the derivative and setting it to zero:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat{p}_i} = -\frac{p_i}{\hat{p}_i} - \mu &= 0 \\ \Rightarrow\; \hat{p}_i &= -\frac{p_i}{\mu} \end{aligned}$$

Since $\sum_i \hat{p}_i = 1$, we have $\sum_i \left(-\dfrac{p_i}{\mu}\right) = 1$, so $\mu = -\sum_i p_i$. Therefore

$$\hat{p}_i = \frac{p_i}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} p_j}$$
