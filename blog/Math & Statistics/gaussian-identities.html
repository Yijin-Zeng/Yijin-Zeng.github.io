Gaussian Identities

Mathematics

A concise handwritten version of this note is available as a PDF.

Gaussian distribution is one of the most important distributions in statistics and machine learning. Its popularity and importance comes not only from the Central Limit Theorem (CLT) but also from its mathematically properties, and its ability to produce closed-form results. Here we discuss a few of its mathematical properties and and derive some of the most practically useful results, such as the conditional distribution.

Univariate Gaussian Distribution

The density function for a univariate Gaussian distribution is

$$f(x; \mu, \sigma) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$$

Showing It Is a Proper Distribution

Instead of solving $\int f(x;\mu,\sigma)\,dx$ directly, we use a multiplication trick: pair the integral with a second copy of itself (over a dummy variable $y$) and switch to polar coordinates.

$$\begin{aligned} \int_{\mathbb{R}} f(x;\mu,\sigma)\,dx \cdot \int_{\mathbb{R}} f(y;\mu,\sigma)\,dy &= \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2} \int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}} \exp\!\left( -\frac{(x-\mu)^2+(y-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right) dx\, dy \end{aligned}$$

Let $(x-\mu) = r \cos\theta$, $(y-\mu) = r \sin\theta$.

$$J = \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(r,\theta)} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -r \sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & r \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}$$ $$|J| = r \cos^2\theta + r \sin^2\theta = r$$

Hence

$$\begin{aligned} \int_{\mathbb{R}} f(x;\mu,\sigma)\,dx \cdot \int_{\mathbb{R}} f(y;\mu,\sigma)\,dy &= \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2} \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^\infty \exp\!\left(-\frac{r^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) r\, dr\, d\theta \\ &= \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2} \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^\infty \frac{1}{2} \exp\!\left(-\frac{r^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) d(r^2)\, d\theta \\ &= \frac{1}{4\pi\sigma^2} \int_0^{2\pi} \left[ -2\sigma^2 \exp\!\left(-\frac{r^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \right]_{r=0}^{\infty} d\theta \\ &= \frac{1}{4\pi\sigma^2} \int_0^{2\pi} 2\sigma^2\, d\theta \\ &= \frac{1}{4\pi\sigma^2} \cdot 4\pi\sigma^2 = 1 \end{aligned}$$

Both factors in the product are the same integral (just with the variable renamed), and both are positive, so if their product is 1, each one individually must equal 1.

Deriving Its Moment Generating Function

To derive the moment generating function of the normal distribution, we use another trick by completing squares.

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}(e^{tX}) &= \int e^{tx} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) dx \\ &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \int \exp\!\left( tx - \frac{x^2 + \mu^2 - 2\mu x}{2\sigma^2} \right) dx \\ &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \int \exp\!\left( -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \left( x^2 - 2\mu x - 2\sigma^2 t x + \mu^2 \right) \right) dx \\ &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \int \exp\!\left( -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \left( (x - (\mu+\sigma^2 t))^2 - \sigma^4 t^2 - 2\mu\sigma^2 t \right) \right) dx \\ &= \exp\!\left( \frac{\sigma^2 t^2}{2} + \mu t \right) \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \int \exp\!\left( -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}(x-(\mu+\sigma^2 t))^2 \right) dx \\ &= \exp\!\left( \frac{\sigma^2 t^2}{2} + \mu t \right) \end{aligned}$$

If $\mu = 0$, $\sigma = 1$, we have $\mathbb{E}(e^{tX}) = e^{t^2/2}$. Differentiating this MGF successively at $t = 0$ gives the raw moments of the standard normal:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}(X) &= \left( t e^{t^2/2} \right)\Big|_{t=0} = 0 \\ \mathbb{E}(X^2) &= \left( e^{t^2/2} + t^2 e^{t^2/2} \right)\Big|_{t=0} = 1 \\ \mathbb{E}(X^3) &= \left( t e^{t^2/2} + 2t e^{t^2/2} + t^3 e^{t^2/2} \right)\Big|_{t=0} = 0 \\ \mathbb{E}(X^4) &= \left( e^{t^2/2} + t^2 e^{t^2/2} + 2e^{t^2/2} + 2t^2 e^{t^2/2} + 3t^2 e^{t^2/2} + t^4 e^{t^2/2} \right)\Big|_{t=0} = 3 \end{aligned}$$

Multivariate Gaussian Distribution

The density function for a multivariate Gaussian distribution is

$$f(x; \mu, \Sigma) = (2\pi)^{-n/2} \det(\Sigma)^{-1/2} \exp\!\left( -\frac{1}{2} (x-\mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (x-\mu) \right)$$

Showing It Is a Proper Distribution

As we did for the univariate case, we first show the density function allows for a proper distribution. We will see that many derivations for the general multivariate Gaussian rely on the fact that any arbitrary Gaussian distribution can be expressed as an affine transformation of a standard normal random vector (i.e., one with an identity covariance matrix).

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal{I} &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} \exp\!\left( -\frac{1}{2} (x-\mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (x-\mu) \right) dx \\ &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} \exp\!\left( -\frac{1}{2} y^\top \Sigma^{-1} y \right) dy \qquad (y = x - \mu) \end{aligned}$$

Since $\Sigma$ is a valid covariance matrix, we must have $\Sigma = S\Lambda S^\top$, where $S$ is orthonormal and $\Lambda$ is diagonal with $\lambda_i > 0$. So $\Sigma^{-1} = S\Lambda^{-1}S^\top$, and

$$y^\top \Sigma^{-1} y = y^\top S \Lambda^{-1} S^\top y = z^\top \Lambda^{-1} z, \qquad \text{where } z = S^\top y$$

Because $S$ is an orthogonal matrix, $\partial z/\partial y = S$, so $|\det(S)| = 1$ (since $SS^\top = I \Rightarrow |S||S^\top| = 1 \Rightarrow \det(S) = \pm 1$). So we have

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal{I} &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} \exp\!\left( -\frac{1}{2} z^\top \Lambda^{-1} z \right) dz \\ &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} \exp\!\left( \sum_i -\frac{z_i^2}{2\lambda_i} \right) dz_1 \cdots dz_n \\ &= \prod_i \sqrt{2\pi\lambda_i} \\ &= (2\pi)^{n/2} |\Sigma|^{1/2} \qquad \text{since } |\Sigma| = |S||\Lambda||S^\top| = |\Lambda| = \prod_i \lambda_i \end{aligned}$$

This makes $f$ integrate to $(2\pi)^{-n/2}|\Sigma|^{-1/2} \cdot (2\pi)^{n/2}|\Sigma|^{1/2} = 1$. As discussed, the change of variables uses the fact that any Gaussian distribution can be expressed as an affine transformation of a standard normal random vector:

  • if $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)$, then $\Sigma^{1/2}Z + \mu \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma)$;
  • on the other hand, if $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma)$, then $\Sigma^{-1/2}(X-\mu) \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)$.

Mean and Variance of the Multivariate Gaussian

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}(X) &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} (2\pi)^{-n/2} \det(\Sigma)^{-1/2}\, x \exp\!\left( -\frac{1}{2} (x-\mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (x-\mu) \right) dx \\ &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} (2\pi)^{-n/2} \det(\Sigma)^{-1/2} (y+\mu) \exp\!\left( -\frac{1}{2} y^\top \Sigma^{-1} y \right) dy \qquad (y = x-\mu) \\ &= \mu + \underbrace{\int_{\mathbb{R}^n} (2\pi)^{-n/2} \det(\Sigma)^{-1/2}\, y \exp\!\left( -\frac{1}{2} y^\top \Sigma^{-1} y \right) dy}_{= 0 \text{ due to symmetry}} \\ &= \mu \end{aligned}$$

For the covariance, $\mathrm{Cov}(X) = \mathbb{E}((X-\mu)(X-\mu)^\top)$:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{Cov}(X) &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} (x-\mu)(x-\mu)^\top (2\pi)^{-n/2} \det(\Sigma)^{-1/2} \exp\!\left( -\frac{1}{2} (x-\mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (x-\mu) \right) dx \\ &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} y y^\top (2\pi)^{-n/2} \det(\Sigma)^{-1/2} \exp\!\left( -\frac{1}{2} y^\top \Sigma^{-1} y \right) dy \qquad (y = x-\mu) \end{aligned}$$

Let $z = \Sigma^{-1/2} y$, so $y = \Sigma^{1/2} z$:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{Cov}(X) &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} \Sigma^{1/2} z z^\top \Sigma^{1/2} (2\pi)^{-n/2} \det(\Sigma)^{-1/2} \exp\!\left( -\frac{1}{2} z^\top z \right) |\det(\Sigma^{1/2})|\, dz \\ &= \Sigma^{1/2} \underbrace{\left( \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} z z^\top (2\pi)^{-n/2} \exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2} z^\top z\right) dz \right)}_{\text{all independent now, this integral} = I} \Sigma^{1/2} \\ &= \Sigma^{1/2}\, I\, \Sigma^{1/2} \\ &= \Sigma \end{aligned}$$

Conditional Distribution of the Multivariate Gaussian

A major advantage of the Gaussian distribution over other distributions is that its conditional distributions are also Gaussian and can be derived in closed form. To be more spefici, if $(X,Y) \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left( \begin{pmatrix} \mu_x \\ \mu_y \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} \Sigma_{xx} & \Sigma_{xy} \\ \Sigma_{yx} & \Sigma_{yy} \end{pmatrix} \right)$, then what is $Y|X$?

$$f_{X,Y}(x,y; \mu,\Sigma) = (2\pi)^{-n/2} \det(\Sigma)^{-1/2} \exp\!\left( -\frac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} x-\mu_x \\ y-\mu_y \end{pmatrix}^\top \Sigma^{-1} \begin{pmatrix} x-\mu_x \\ y-\mu_y \end{pmatrix} \right)$$

Let $\Lambda = \Sigma^{-1}$ be the precision matrix. (This reuses the letter $\Lambda$, which was the diagonal eigenvalue matrix in the eigendecomposition $\Sigma = S\Lambda S^\top$ above. Here $\Lambda$ means the precision matrix instead, which is $\Sigma^{-1} = (S\Lambda S^\top)^{-1} = S\Lambda^{-1}S^\top$ in terms of the old one.) It is positive-definite as well:

$$\Lambda = \begin{pmatrix} \Lambda_{xx} & \Lambda_{xy} \\ \Lambda_{yx} & \Lambda_{yy} \end{pmatrix}$$

where $\Lambda_{xx}$ and $\Lambda_{yy}$ are symmetric, and $\Lambda_{xy} = \Lambda_{yx}^\top$.

Expanding the joint density's quadratic form in block form:

$$\begin{aligned} &-\frac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} x-\mu_x \\ y-\mu_y \end{pmatrix}^\top \Lambda \begin{pmatrix} x-\mu_x \\ y-\mu_y \end{pmatrix} \\ &= -\frac{1}{2} \Big( (x-\mu_x)^\top \Lambda_{xx} (x-\mu_x) + (y-\mu_y)^\top \Lambda_{yx} (x-\mu_x) \\ &\qquad\quad + (x-\mu_x)^\top \Lambda_{xy} (y-\mu_y) + (y-\mu_y)^\top \Lambda_{yy} (y-\mu_y) \Big) \end{aligned}$$

Now note that the conditional density $f_{Y|X}(y|x;\mu,\Sigma) = f_{X,Y}(x,y;\mu,\Sigma) / f_X(x;\mu,\Sigma)$ is a function of $y$ only, given $x$. So we expand the exponent again, this time keeping only the terms that depend on $y$ and folding everything else into a constant:

$$\begin{aligned} &-\frac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} x-\mu_x \\ y-\mu_y \end{pmatrix}^\top \Lambda \begin{pmatrix} x-\mu_x \\ y-\mu_y \end{pmatrix} \\ &= -\frac{1}{2} \Big( y^\top \Lambda_{yy} y - y^\top \Lambda_{yy} \mu_y - \mu_y^\top \Lambda_{yy} y + y^\top \Lambda_{yx} (x-\mu_x) + (x-\mu_x)^\top \Lambda_{xy} y + \text{const} \Big) \\ &= -\frac{1}{2} \Big( y^\top \Lambda_{yy} y + 2y^\top \big( \Lambda_{yx}(x-\mu_x) - \Lambda_{yy} \mu_y \big) \Big) + \text{const} \\ &= -\frac{1}{2} \Big( y^\top \Lambda_{yy} y + 2y^\top \Lambda_{yy} \big( \Lambda_{yy}^{-1} \Lambda_{yx}(x-\mu_x) - \mu_y \big) \Big) + \text{const} \end{aligned}$$

Note that any multivariate Gaussian distribution has the exponent

$$\begin{aligned} -\frac{1}{2} (x-\mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (x-\mu) &= -\frac{1}{2} \left( x^\top \Sigma^{-1} x - x^\top \Sigma^{-1} \mu - \mu^\top \Sigma^{-1} x + \mu^\top \Sigma^{-1} \mu \right) \\ &= -\frac{1}{2} \Big( \underbrace{x^\top \Sigma^{-1} x}_{\text{quadratic term}} - \underbrace{2x^\top \Sigma^{-1} \mu}_{\text{linear term}} \Big) + \text{const} \end{aligned}$$

Matching the quadratic term in $y$ gives $\mathrm{Cov}(Y|X) = \Lambda_{yy}^{-1}$ (and symmetrically $\mathrm{Cov}(X|Y) = \Lambda_{xx}^{-1}$), and matching the linear term gives

$$\mathbb{E}(Y|X) = \mu_y - \Lambda_{yy}^{-1} \Lambda_{yx} (x-\mu_x) \qquad \left(\text{symmetrically: } \mathbb{E}(X|Y) = \mu_x - \Lambda_{xx}^{-1} \Lambda_{xy} (y-\mu_y) \right)$$

Now, the problem is to express the precision matrix $\Lambda = \Sigma^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} \Lambda_{xx} & \Lambda_{xy} \\ \Lambda_{yx} & \Lambda_{yy} \end{pmatrix}$ using $\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} \Sigma_{xx} & \Sigma_{xy} \\ \Sigma_{yx} & \Sigma_{yy} \end{pmatrix}$. To do this, we apply the Schur complement based on Gaussian elimination. For a generic block matrix $\begin{pmatrix} A & B \\ C & D \end{pmatrix}$:

$$\begin{pmatrix} A & B \\ C & D \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} I & 0 \\ -D^{-1}C & I \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} A-BD^{-1}C & B \\ 0 & D \end{pmatrix}$$ $$\begin{pmatrix} I & -BD^{-1} \\ 0 & I \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} A-BD^{-1}C & B \\ 0 & D \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} A-BD^{-1}C & 0 \\ 0 & D \end{pmatrix}$$

So we have

$$\begin{pmatrix} I & -BD^{-1} \\ 0 & I \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} A & B \\ C & D \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} I & 0 \\ -D^{-1}C & I \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} A-BD^{-1}C & 0 \\ 0 & D \end{pmatrix}$$ $$\Rightarrow\; \begin{pmatrix} A & B \\ C & D \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} I & BD^{-1} \\ 0 & I \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} A-BD^{-1}C & 0 \\ 0 & D \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} I & 0 \\ D^{-1}C & I \end{pmatrix}$$ $$\begin{aligned} \Rightarrow\; \begin{pmatrix} A & B \\ C & D \end{pmatrix}^{-1} &= \begin{pmatrix} I & 0 \\ -D^{-1}C & I \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} (A-BD^{-1}C)^{-1} & 0 \\ 0 & D^{-1} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} I & -BD^{-1} \\ 0 & I \end{pmatrix} \\ &= \begin{pmatrix} I & 0 \\ -D^{-1}C & I \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} M & 0 \\ 0 & D^{-1} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} I & -BD^{-1} \\ 0 & I \end{pmatrix} \\ &= \begin{pmatrix} M & 0 \\ -D^{-1}CM & D^{-1} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} I & -BD^{-1} \\ 0 & I \end{pmatrix} \\ &= \begin{pmatrix} M & -MBD^{-1} \\ -D^{-1}CM & D^{-1}CMBD^{-1}+D^{-1} \end{pmatrix} \end{aligned}$$

where $M = (A - BD^{-1}C)^{-1}$

Generating Samples from a Non-identity Gaussian

We have discussed that general multivariate Gaussian is only an affine transformation of a standard normal random vector. Suppose we can sample from $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$. How do we generate $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)$?

Let $Y \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and $Z = \mu + WY$. Then

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}(Z) &= \mu \\ \mathrm{Cov}(Z) &= W\, \mathrm{Cov}(Y)\, W^\top = W W^\top \end{aligned}$$

So if $W W^\top = \Sigma$, then $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)$. The question becomes how to find such a $W$ for a given symmetric positive definite matrix $A$ (here $A = \Sigma$):

  1. Cholesky decomposition. For a real-valued symmetric positive definite matrix $A$, we have $$A = L L^\top$$ where $L$ is a lower triangular matrix with strictly positive diagonal entries.
  2. Eigen-decomposition. Since $A$ is symmetric, we have $$\begin{aligned} A &= Q \Lambda Q^\top \\ &= Q \Lambda^{1/2} \Lambda^{1/2} Q^\top \\ &= Q \Lambda^{1/2} (Q \Lambda^{1/2})^\top \\ &= W W^\top \end{aligned}$$
  3. Symmetric square root. Let $A^{1/2} = Q \Lambda^{1/2} Q^\top$. Then $A = A^{1/2} A^{1/2} = A^{1/2} (A^{1/2})^\top$, and this $W = A^{1/2}$ is symmetric.

If $W$ works, i.e. $W W^\top = \Sigma$, then $WR$ also works as long as $R R^\top = I$, since $W R R^\top W^\top = W W^\top = \Sigma$. So we have infinitely many valid matrices $W$.
